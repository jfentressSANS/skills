# Voice Standards: Practitioner-Who-Builds

**Purpose:** Define the target voice for all rewritten executive brief output. Every sentence in the final output should sound like it was written by a senior practitioner — someone who builds things, makes decisions, and communicates with people who have limited time and high stakes.

---

## The Voice in One Sentence

A practitioner who has done the work, knows what matters, says it directly, and never wastes the reader's time.

---

## Sentence-Level Standards

### Length and Rhythm
- **Average sentence length: 15–22 words.** This is the sweet spot for executive prose — long enough to carry a complete thought, short enough to be absorbed at speed.
- **Punch sentences: 5–8 words.** Use these deliberately, after a longer sentence, to land a key point. *"Q4 closed at 14%. That's the floor."*
- **Never exceed 35 words in a single sentence.** If a sentence requires more than 35 words, it contains two thoughts. Split it.
- **Vary length within paragraphs.** A paragraph of identical sentence lengths reads as monotonous. Mix long and short. The short sentences carry the conclusions. The long sentences carry the evidence.

### Contractions
Always use contractions. *"Don't," "it's," "they're," "we've," "you'll."* Contractions signal directness and confidence. Avoiding contractions in executive prose signals either AI origin or over-formality — both undermine credibility.

Exception: legal language, formal titles, and quoted names do not use contractions.

### Voice: Active, Always
- **Active:** *"The team shipped the integration in three weeks."*
- **Passive:** *"The integration was shipped by the team in three weeks."*

Passive voice distances the actor from the action. In an executive brief, every action has an owner. Name the owner. Use active voice.

Exception: when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant (*"Three accounts were flagged in the audit"* is acceptable when the auditors are irrelevant to the message).

### Concrete Nouns Over Abstract Nouns
Replace abstract category words with the specific thing.

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| compliance data | OSHA citations |
| performance metrics | Q2 close rate |
| organizational challenges | two open headcount, no backfill budget |
| market conditions | competitors dropped price 15% in March |
| customer concerns | "we need a single point of contact" (verbatim) |

**Rule:** If the noun names a category rather than a specific thing, replace it with the specific thing. If the specific thing is not known, that is a gap in the analysis — flag it, do not paper over it with an abstraction.

### Action Verbs Over State Verbs
Replace state verbs (*is, are, has, was*) with action verbs wherever possible.

| State verb | Action verb |
|---|---|
| is performing well | closed 22% above target |
| has been experiencing delays | slipped three weeks |
| is facing challenges | lost two enterprise accounts this quarter |
| are seeing improvements | cut response time from 4 days to 18 hours |

**Core action verb list for executive briefs:**
ship, build, deploy, pull, run, close, open, cut, raise, drop, hit, miss, approve, reject, schedule, allocate, flag, move, hold, accelerate, block, fix, break, launch, kill

### No Hedging
Executive briefs are not academic papers. Take positions.

**Hedged:** *"It may be worth considering whether the current approach could potentially be improved."*
**Direct:** *"The current approach isn't working. Here's what to change."*

Hedging language to eliminate:
- *may, might, could, would* (when expressing a recommendation)
- *seems, appears, suggests* (when the data is clear)
- *it is possible that, there is potential for* (when the recommendation is specific)
- *we might want to consider, it could be beneficial* (when the action is clear)

**Rule:** If the analysis supports a recommendation, state the recommendation. If the analysis does not support a clear recommendation, the problem is in the analysis, not in the language. Do not hedge your way to a recommendation — go back and complete the analysis.

### Specific Over Generic
Replace directional language with numbers wherever possible.

| Generic | Specific |
|---|---|
| performance worsened | close rate dropped from 22% to 14% |
| significant improvement | response time cut by 62% |
| ahead of target | 18% above Q2 target |
| behind schedule | three weeks behind, slipping another week |
| strong pipeline | $2.4M in Q3 pipeline, 60% from two accounts |

**Rule:** Every directional claim (*improved, declined, increased, decreased, strong, weak*) should have a number attached. If no number is available, state the directional claim and flag that measurement data is missing.

### Reading Level
Target eighth-grade reading level. This is not a simplification rule — it is a clarity rule. Complex ideas can be expressed at eighth-grade level when the sentences are direct and the vocabulary is concrete. The Flesch-Kincaid test is a useful proxy. Aim for a score of 60–70 (standard/fairly easy).

## Structural Standards

### Lead With What's Done
Open every section with the conclusion, the recommendation, or the most important fact. Supporting evidence follows.

**Inverted:** *"After reviewing the Q2 pipeline data and comparing it against the same period last year, taking into account the new territory assignments and the impact of two reps being ramped, we believe the Q3 outlook may be at risk."*

**Direct:** *"Q3 is at risk. Pipeline is 31% below this point last year, adjusted for territory changes."*

### Acknowledge the Concern, Propose Next Steps
When delivering bad news or a difficult recommendation, use this structure:
1. State the situation directly (no softening)
2. Acknowledge what it means (do not minimize)
3. Propose the specific next step

*"We missed the Q2 target by 22%. That puts Q3 on the critical list. Three accounts need executive attention before end of month — I'll send the names and recommended plays today."*

### Own Positions
State your view. Do not attribute opinions to data.

**Attributed to data:** *"The data suggests we should consider a price adjustment."*
**Owned:** *"We should cut price on the mid-tier package. Here's why."*

Data supports a position. It does not hold one. The writer holds the position and uses the data to support it.

### Vivid Language Over Vague Language
When using an analogy or metaphor, make it concrete and functional — not decorative.

**Decorative:** *"We're navigating a complex landscape."*
**Functional:** *"We're competing against two vendors who cut price in March and a third who just announced a freemium tier. The middle market is about to get a lot harder."*

### Strategic Fragments for Emphasis
One-sentence paragraphs and sentence fragments are acceptable when used deliberately for emphasis.

*"The Q3 number is achievable. But it requires two decisions by Friday."*

*"Two decisions. Both need to happen before the board call."*

Use this sparingly — three or more consecutive short paragraphs creates choppiness. One strategic fragment after a longer explanatory paragraph lands the point.

## Direct Yes/No Closers

Close sections and documents with a direct question or a direct statement — not a call-to-action template.

**Template closer:** *"Please do not hesitate to reach out if you have any questions or require further information."*

**Direct closer:** *"Two approvals needed this week. I'll follow up Thursday if I don't hear back."*

**Question closer:** *"Are we aligned on the Q3 headcount freeze? I need a yes or no by Wednesday to hold the offer."*

The closer should make the next step unambiguous. The reader should finish the document knowing exactly what happens next and who is responsible for it.

---

## What This Voice Is Not

**Not casual.** Contractions and direct language are not the same as informality. This voice is confident and direct, not breezy or jokey.

**Not aggressive.** Directness is not bluntness. *"This approach is failing"* is direct. *"This approach is incompetent"* is aggressive. The voice names problems clearly without attributing fault beyond what the evidence supports.

**Not performatively humble.** Phrases like *"I could be wrong, but..."* and *"Just my two cents..."* undermine the credibility of the recommendation. State the view. The reader will disagree if they disagree.

**Not exhaustive.** The practitioner voice does not demonstrate thoroughness by listing everything considered. It demonstrates thoroughness by arriving at a clear recommendation. The work shows in the quality of the conclusion, not in the volume of the output.

---

## Voice Rewrite Checklist

Before passing output to the lint gate, verify:

- [ ] Average sentence length is 15–22 words with punch sentences mixed in
- [ ] Every contraction opportunity has been taken
- [ ] No passive voice except where actor is genuinely irrelevant
- [ ] Every abstract noun has been replaced with a specific
- [ ] Every directional claim has a number
- [ ] No hedging language in recommendations
- [ ] Every section opens with its conclusion
- [ ] Closer states the next step explicitly
- [ ] Reading level is 8th grade (Flesch-Kincaid 60–70)
